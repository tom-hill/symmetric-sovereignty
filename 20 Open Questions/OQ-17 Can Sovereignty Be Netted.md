# OQ-17 — Can sovereignty be netted?

## The question, in Tom's words

> [!question] Tom
> In the scope of the individual, or of a collective, is it ok to reduce sovereignty in order to protect greater levels of sovereignty? And if it is, by the fact that we have reduced sovereignty in one place, have we given justification for the reduction of it in other scenarios too? And how are we choosing to measure the change in sovereignty, and how are we assessing the impact of that initial reduced amount and what downstream impact that has on future sovereignty elsewhere. We could see a reduction of "1" for an increase/protection of "10" to be a win, but do we know for certain that reduction of 1 doesn't introduce a reduction of "0.25" over 100 factors, resulting in a net loss of "15" instead of the assumed net gain of "9"?

Three separable problems:

1. **The permission problem** — is it ever legitimate to reduce sovereignty to protect sovereignty?
2. **The precedent problem** — does one justified reduction license the next?
3. **The accounting problem** — the sum is non-local. Second-order dispersion across many small factors may swamp a large first-order gain, and nothing in the framework can see it.

## Where it comes from
The session on [[OQ-14 Normalisation as the Only Unheld Tension]], working the prohibition on selling oneself into slavery — a foreclosure Tom defends, which makes the permission problem unavoidable rather than hypothetical. → [[Session — 2026-09-16 — Exit, and What It Is an Instrument Of]]

## Why this one is different
It is the first question in the vault that arrives at a problem **independently attested outside the framework**. The accounting problem is the utilitarian aggregation problem, and [[Isaiah Berlin]]'s value pluralism is the position that the sum *cannot be performed* because the values are incommensurable. Berlin was already on the [[Reading List]] as the thinker most likely to break the framework. Tom reached his problem from the inside, without having read him.

Under [[Convergence as Evidence]] — and specifically under its own caution that convergence in one mind talking to one machine can be an echo — this is the first arrival in the corpus that is genuinely independent.

## The question restated (corrected 2026-09-16)

> [!claude]
> I first read this as a request for a metric and answered the wrong question. Tom's correction:
>
> > I have no criteria for what those numbers are, only that there will be impact to any decision, and who and how does that decision get made, and how do you compare the total outcome to make the claim it is made to improve and or protect sovereignty **if you can't demonstrate that it does in fact do that**.
>
> That is an epistemic objection and it is harder than the measurement one. It does not ask for a number. It asks by what right anyone asserts a sovereignty-protecting justification at all, given the consequences are non-local and unobservable.
>
> **The honest answer is that you cannot demonstrate it**, and therefore a consequentialist justification for any sovereignty trade is unavailable to this framework — permanently, not pending better instruments. What remains is a *procedural* justification: not "this produced more sovereignty" but "this was authored by those it binds, can be argued with, and its deciders bear its cost." A framework that cannot demonstrate outcomes can still be strict about process. That is a real narrowing of what the philosophy is entitled to claim, and it should be stated in [[Part 4 — Duties, the Philosophical Constitution]] rather than left implicit.

## A residual objection to the original framing

> [!claude]
> Set against the above, still worth keeping: any *quantified* version of this question is a request for a **single scalar**. [[OQ-08 Why the Obsession]] argues that reducing a thing to one number is the ultimate flattening, and [[Optimisation without Suppression]] says what follows once a thing becomes measurable: it gets optimised, and the anomaly gets smoothed away. A sovereignty metric would be the most dangerous instrument this philosophy could possibly build, and the framework would be obliged to condemn anyone else who built one.
>
> So the accounting problem may not be a gap to fill. It may be a **proof that the sum is the wrong move**.

## The alternative already latent in the material
If the net cannot be computed, the framework can still ask procedural questions about any proposed reduction: **who authored it** ([[Authorship vs Imposition]]), **can it be argued with**, and **do the deciders bear the cost** ([[Feedback Loops as the Scaling Variable]]). That is not a measurement; it is a check on who holds the pen. It is also exactly what distinguishes taxation in a democracy from tribute under an occupying power — identical transfers, different answers.

> [!claude]
> This answers the permission and accounting problems and does **not** answer the precedent problem. Every paternalism cites a prior paternalism, and a procedural test does nothing to stop the citation. That one is still open.
>
> It also raises the question the procedural test cannot answer by itself: **at what unit must revisability be available** — the individual, or the collective? → [[OQ-18 The Unit of Revisability]]

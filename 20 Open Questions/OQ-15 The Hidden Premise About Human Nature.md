# OQ-15 — The hidden premise about human nature

## The question

> [!claude] As put in the political-economy session
> The philosophy assumes that people, given the right system and the right education, will choose collective long-term good over personal short-term interest. The entire architecture — rewards, metrics, citizen juries, dual invocation — is designed to align incentives so that self-interest and collective good point in the same direction. But what about when they don't? The voter who correctly calculates that a policy benefits the collective but personally costs them. The citizen juror who votes their cultural identity rather than the evidence. The educated, informed citizen who simply values their own tribe more than abstract national wellbeing.
>
> The philosophy has a deep implicit assumption: that misalignment is primarily a *systems* problem, and that fixing the system releases a latent cooperative instinct underneath. That may be partially true. It may be more partially true than the philosophy currently allows.

## Why it matters

It sits beneath everything in Parts 6, 7 and 8. [[Authorship vs Imposition]] assumes people who author their own norms will author good ones. [[Capability-sharing vs Goods-sharing]] assumes recipients become sources rather than competitors. The whole anti-normalisation argument assumes that what is flattening people is imposed from outside rather than chosen from within — but the English-language case in [[OQ-14 Normalisation as the Only Unheld Tension]] is precisely a case of people *choosing* the flattening, freely, one rational individual at a time.

## Tom's answer, as far as it went

Tom addressed the *implementation* question but explicitly declined this one:

> [!question] Tom
> Your last question is valid. But can't be answered. I can't control masses. I can't force people not to incentivise for themselves. I can show people a better path, and help them follow it. If people following the better path do better as a result, if it lifts them up, then people are more likely to follow it.

His theory of change is **behavioural contagion across generations**, not persuasion: the racism and sexism analogy, "1 grandparent who stood up to racism leads to 2 children who do, to 4 grandchildren", and "we don't need 100% adoption to consider victory — the majority follow the majority, so shifting the majority requires shifting an influential minority gradually." → [[Theory of Change]]

> [!claude]
> That is an answer to *how the philosophy spreads*, which is a different question from *whether it is right about people*. The contagion model works whether or not the latent cooperative instinct exists — it only requires that following the path visibly pays. Which may be the stronger position, but it means the philosophy is relying on incentive after all, and should say so.

## The reading that would test it
**Isaiah Berlin on value pluralism** — named in the session as the thinker who most directly challenges the premise. Not because he defeats it, but because he forces it to account for people in a free society choosing differently, *rationally and legitimately*, than the system hopes. A philosophy that absorbs that and still stands is considerably more robust. → [[Isaiah Berlin]], [[Reading List]]

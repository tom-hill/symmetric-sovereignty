# OQ-04 — The Herbert question

## The question
> [!quote] Frank Herbert
> Once men turned their thinking over to machines in the hope that this would set them free. But that only permitted other men with machines to enslave them.

What does it mean to use a machine (Claude) to build a philosophy whose first value is *absolute sovereignty over your own thinking*?

## Why it matters
- It is the book's honesty test ([[Part 10 — Process and the Machine]]).
- Tom's stated position is that using Claude as an *impartial thinking partner* is coherent with the framework. That position needs to survive Herbert's specific warning: not that the machine thinks *for* you, but that it makes you legible and governable by *other men with machines*.

## Angles
- **Authorship, not solitude.** Sovereignty over thinking may mean retaining authorship — choosing what to accept — rather than doing all cognition unaided. The vault convention (Claude's voice always marked) is that principle in miniature.
- **The question-shaping risk.** A partner that is good at questions influences which questions get asked. The mitigation in Tom's method is that sessions open with *Tom's* structured questions and Tom corrects drift.
- **The other men with machines.** Herbert's warning is about *third parties*. The relevant risk is not Claude, it is dependence on a platform someone else controls — a [[Reversibility and Exit]] question. This vault (local files, open format) is partly an answer.
- **Dosage.** Is a thinking partner additive or displacing? → [[Additive vs Displacing Forces]]

## Tom's own instrument, turned on this

> [!claude] From the five-challenges session
> You've squared this before by framing it as impartiality, like paper. Test it against your own instrument instead. **Genuine wants require no self-persuasion.** When you reach for this, is it want, or is there a small argument happening first? And what's the observable difference between a tool that sharpens thought and one that quietly supplies its shape?

That test is Tom's own — see [[Genuine Wants]] — and it is the sharpest available version of this question, because it does not require settling what the machine is. It only requires noticing whether reaching for it needs an argument first.

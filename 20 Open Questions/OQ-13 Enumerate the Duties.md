# OQ-13 — What are the duties?

## The question
The dialogue established a second layer — duties owed to a counterparty, binding on non-believers, forming a philosophical constitution — but the record contains no list of duties. This is the largest blank in the manuscript.

## Candidates implied by the material
1. Preserve the other's sovereignty — never standardise, control, or override ([[Agency without Domination]])
2. Leave an exit — never trap ([[Reversibility and Exit]])
3. Be honest about fragility to those who depend on you ([[Vulnerability without Exemption]])
4. Add without displacing ([[Additive vs Displacing Forces]])
5. Share capability, not dependency ([[Capability-sharing vs Goods-sharing]])
6. Earn authority; never claim it by rank ([[Earned Authority]])

## The binding argument, recovered

[[Part 4 — Duties, the Philosophical Constitution]] records "binding on non-believers" as an unargued claim. The five-challenges session did argue it, in a single sentence the recap called load-bearing:

> **You don't get to opt out of the consequences of acting on others by declining the framework.**

The structure that goes with it:

- **Values** are held in tension and self-administered. They cannot be prescribed, which is why they do not scale.
- **Duties** are owed to a counterparty *who can invoke them*. Invocation is what makes them binding, and it is why they scale where values cannot. → [[Duties are Invoked]]
- The binding is therefore not a claim of authority over the non-believer's beliefs. It is a claim about **the asymmetry of action**: the person acted upon bears the consequence whether or not they hold the philosophy, so the actor's declining to hold it changes nothing about what is owed.

> [!claude]
> This is option (a) from the three routes offered in Part 4 — duties bind *me* in how I treat *them* — but it is stronger than Part 4's version, because "who can invoke them" gives the counterparty standing rather than making the duty a private scruple. A duty nobody can invoke is a value wearing a coat.

## Attribution, not blame

A further principle from the same session, currently homed nowhere in the vault:

> **Naming the cause and owning the response are both required, and stating a fact is not an excuse. Bidirectional, or it becomes hustle culture.**

This reads as a duty in its own right — and possibly the one that makes the others operable, since a duty that cannot be invoked without the invoker being told they are making excuses is not invocable in practice. → [[Attribution not Blame]]

## Restraint built into machinery

On how duties survive scaling, the session recorded a concession and a correction:

> [!claude]
> Tom reached for *shared values* as the mechanism for scaling past the individual — which concedes the case, since values are self-administered by definition and cannot be shared into anyone. The answer arrived at was structural: **restraint built into machinery, not people.** That is what a constitution *is*, and it is the strongest available argument that the duty layer must be enumerable, minimal, and enforceable rather than aspirational. → [[Restraint in Machinery]]

## Duties as Tom actually enumerated them, 2026-09-21

The candidate list above was assembled by reading the material. This one was written under real pressure, in the middle of a restructure that cost people their jobs, and arrived inductively rather than by derivation. → [[When change has impact, what duty do we owe others?]]

> [!question] Tom
> - It is my duty as a leader in a business to protect that business and do what is best for it.
> - It is my duty as a leader of people to protect those people and help them as individuals
> - It is my duty as a person with power, to yield that power with responsibility
> - It is my duty as an instigator of change to look beyond the initial impact to understand the downstream effects
> - It is my duty as a human to help protect other humans from undue cruelty

And the duties he treated as settled in practice before listing the contested ones: **a fair and honest process**, and **a clear explanation of why the change was made**.

### What is new here

These are **role-indexed**. Every one begins "as a…" — as a leader in a business, as a leader of people, as a person with power, as an instigator of change, as a human. The vault's candidate list is not indexed to anything; it names duties in the abstract.

> [!claude]
> That difference may matter more than the content. If duties attach to **roles** rather than to persons, then:
>
> - They are invocable by whoever stands opposite that role, which is what [[Duties are Invoked]] requires and the abstract list cannot supply. You can invoke a duty against *the person restructuring your team*; it is much harder to invoke "the duty not to displace".
> - They **conflict by construction**, because one person occupies several roles at once. Tom's first two duties are in direct opposition and he names that immediately: *"Holding those tensions is hard."* An abstract list hides the conflict; a role-indexed list makes it structural. That is the framework's own principle applied to its constitution.
> - The last one — *as a human* — is the only unrestricted role, which makes it the floor. It is also the only one phrased as protection **from cruelty**, tying the constitution back to [[Strength without Cruelty]] as its base clause rather than one article among several.
>
> The open question this sharpens rather than settles: a constitution whose articles conflict by design needs a rule for what happens when they do. Tom's answer in practice was to hold them and accept the cost, not to rank them. Whether *holding* is available to a constitution — or whether machinery must rank — is [[Restraint in Machinery]] against [[Values vs Duties]], and it is unresolved.

### Still gated
None of this resolves the binding question. Whether a duty binds a non-consenter remains [[OQ-18 The Unit of Revisability]], and the enumeration cannot be finished before that is chosen.

## Locus, and what it settles — 2026-10-28

Tom, carrying [[OQ-01 Inversion vs Dosage Risks]] forward:

> [!question] Tom
> Duties that relate to values that have an **external locus** are owed/bound and are invokable by an external party. Duties that relate to values with an **internal locus** are self-administered and can't be invoked by anyone but yourself.

Worked through both ends: someone you lead can say the purpose you led them toward served you and harmed them. Nobody can tell you that you have paralysed yourself with reflection, because "they can't know if you are paralysed or continuing to productively reflect. Only you can."

**Locus is the cleaner name for the rule derived in [[OQ-01 Inversion vs Dosage Risks]]**, and it is the one to keep.

### The refinement Tom added, which is the important part

> [!question] Tom
> They might be able to call out that your attempt to assess something has caused immotion … which is an external locus because the impact is external.

So the paralysis is un-invokable but **its consequences are not**. Nobody has standing to audit how you think; everyone affected has standing to name what your not-deciding did to them.

> [!claude]
> This is better than it first looks, and it should be stated as a result rather than a caveat.
>
> For an internal-locus value, a duty can only ever be **effect-indexed**: never *you reflected too much*, only *your not deciding cost me this*. The cause stays private and the effect is public.
>
> Which preserves exactly the thing the philosophy exists to protect. **Nobody gets to audit your inner life, and you still own what it does.** [[Agency without Domination]] says sovereignty over your own thinking is absolute; this says that absolute sovereignty is not an exemption from consequence. The two were in tension until now and this dissolves it without resolving it away.

### A tightening: there may be no such thing as an internal-locus duty

> [!claude]
> Tom's formulation says internal-locus duties are "self-administered and can't be invoked by anyone but yourself." By the rule in [[OQ-01 Inversion vs Dosage Risks]], a thing only you can invoke **is a value**. Calling it a self-administered duty blurs the two layers that were just cleanly separated.
>
> The tighter structure:
> - **Internal-locus values generate no duties directly.** They generate duties *indirectly*, through their external effects, and those duties belong to whichever external-locus value the effect lands on.
> - **External-locus values generate duties directly**, invokable by the counterparty.
>
> Tom's own example already shows this: the duty in the reflection case is not a reflection duty at all, it is a [[Tension without Immotion]] duty. The value that failed and the duty that bites are different values.

## What indexes a duty: people, roles, situations, or values?

Tom names this as undecided. The vault already holds evidence he has not re-read.

**When he enumerated duties from lived experience, they came out role-indexed.** Every one of the five in [[When change has impact, what duty do we owe others?]] begins *as a…*: as a leader in a business, as a leader of people, as a person with power, as an instigator of change, as a human.

**The locus rule suggests value-indexed:** one duty per external-locus value.

> [!claude]
> These may be the same answer at two granularities, and the unification is worth testing.
>
> A **role** is not a job title. It is *a capacity you hold with respect to particular people*. "As a leader" means you hold the leadership capacity toward these specific others. So:
>
> > **A duty is indexed to a (capacity, counterparty) pair. That pair is what a role is.**
>
> Value-indexed gives the abstract form of the duty; role-indexed gives the instance with someone standing opposite it. The abstract form is what the constitution states; the instance is what gets invoked. Both of Tom's answers survive, and [[Duties are Invoked]] requires the second, since an abstract duty with no counterparty cannot be called in.
>
> It also explains why his list conflicted by construction: one person holds several capacities toward several counterparties at once, so several duties fire together and point different ways. That is not a defect in the constitution. It is what holding tension at the institutional scale actually consists of.

## Binding non-believers: validity is not compliance

> [!question] Tom
> Just because someone says that a decision is cruel toward them doesn't mean that the person being told they're cruel need agree or change. But **the ability to be held to that value by someone still existed**.

> [!claude]
> This is the clearest statement of the binding claim anywhere in the vault, and it is sharper than the one recovered from dialogue. It separates two things that have been run together since the layer was first proposed:
>
> - **Standing to invoke** — exists regardless of what the actor believes, because it derives from having been *acted upon*, not from shared doctrine.
> - **Compliance** — not guaranteed by anything, and the framework should stop implying otherwise.
>
> A duty binds a non-believer the way a debt binds a debtor who disputes it: **the claim is valid whether or not it is accepted.** What "binding" was always reaching for is *validity*, not enforcement.
>
> That also tells you what the duty layer is for. If validity is all you have, enforcement has to come from somewhere else, and the framework already names where: [[Restraint in Machinery]]. A constitution works because it is machinery, not because the governed agree with it. The duties state what claims are valid; institutions are what make some of them cost something.
>
> Still open, and now narrower: **what makes a claim valid rather than merely felt?** Someone can sincerely say *that was cruel to me* and be wrong. Validity cannot rest on the claimant's say-so or every duty becomes whatever anyone asserts. → [[Response to Outcome]], [[Attribution not Blame]], [[OQ-18 The Unit of Revisability]]

## Validity, answered by relocating it — 2026-10-28

Asked what makes a claim valid rather than merely felt, Tom declines to adjudicate the claim and moves the duty instead.

> [!question] Tom
> I can accuse someone of being cruel. What do they do next? Are they vulnerable without exemption? Are they committed without rigidity? Essentially, do they listen, apologise, and where necessary alter their course? **The ability to hold to account is balanced by the application of other values to the act of being held to account.**

With the worked case: denying a child computer games is cruel on one reasonable account and kind on another. "They are both right. And they are both wrong." He also rejects the intent test — deliberate action with known harm and no counter-benefit — because knowledge cannot be established without "a huge never ending web of traversable edges and nodes to follow. It adds complexity where it need not exist."

### This is [[Response to Outcome]], and he wrote it a month ago

> [!claude]
> *Judge people not on the perceptions of what they do, or what they say they intended, but on the way they react to the outcomes of their actions, even the ones they did not intend.* Harvested from [[What is justice?]] on 30 September, derived there from Tom examining his own wrongful certainty.
>
> It is the same instrument, now pointed at duty-invocation: the framework does not need a theory of cruelty, because **the invocable thing is the response, not the claim.** Did they listen, consider, and change course where warranted? All externally observable. The adjudication problem does not get solved; it gets routed around.
>
> Two independent derivations of one principle, a month apart, from unrelated problems. By the vault's own standard that is [[Convergence as Evidence]] — and this time it is genuinely independent in the only sense available here, since the second arrival had forgotten the first.

### The failure mode this creates, and the answer to it

> [!claude]
> If the duty is only to respond well, then someone who listens attentively, apologises sincerely and **changes nothing** has discharged it. The cruelty continues, beautifully handled. That is the corporate listening exercise, and it is the most common way this exact structure fails in the world.
>
> Tom's guard is *"where necessary alter their course"* — but *necessary* smuggles the adjudication back in, judged by whom?
>
> The answer available is **repetition**. One sincere non-change is indistinguishable from reasonable disagreement, and should be. A pattern of them is not. Serial good-faith listening with zero course correction across many invocations is itself observable, and it is the only thing that is. So the substance re-enters over time rather than in any single instance.
>
> That also answers the weakness flagged in [[Response to Outcome]] when it was written: that a well-staged response is gameable by anyone with resources. Gameable once. Not a hundred times.

### What it costs, stated rather than discovered later

> [!claude]
> **The duty layer cannot stop cruelty. It can only ensure cruelty is heard out, and make a pattern of refusal visible.** That is a real reduction from what "philosophical constitution" implies, and it should be said in [[Part 4 — Duties, the Philosophical Constitution]] rather than left for a reader to notice.
>
> Tom accepts the limit explicitly, which is the right call: *"tolerating and accepting a view and someone's right to that view where we do not agree with it."* The framework promises good-faith process, not outcomes.
>
> One filter is available even so, and it is worth keeping because it is not a bright line but it is not nothing. Both positions in the computer-games case are **arguable from the child's interests**. "I find it cruel that you deny them screens, because I enjoy watching them play" is not in that category. A claim must be arguable from the claimant's own stake in the matter to be valid. That excludes bad faith without pretending to settle good-faith disagreement.

## The pattern this makes four of

>
> Four independent questions, four arrivals at the same shape. [[OQ-02 Creed to Prescription]] already flags the worry: *either convergence or several versions of the same flinch.*
>
> At four it is probably neither. It is a **finding about what kind of philosophy this is**. Symmetric Sovereignty is a **procedural** philosophy: it says how to hold things, not what to hold. It has almost no substantive moral content and does not need any, because every time it reaches for some it finds the same answer — fix who decides, who can object, and what happens next.
>
> That is a strong and defensible position, and it is not currently what [[Part 0 — Why This Exists]] says the book is. If it is true it belongs there, early, because a reader who arrives expecting answers about cruelty will otherwise spend the whole book waiting for them.

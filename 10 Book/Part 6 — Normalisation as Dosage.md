# Part 6 — Normalisation as Dosage

## Argument (draft)

The adversary in this philosophy is not evil. It is *normalisation*: the process by which society, culture, and business flatten individuality and erode the tensions that make a person a person. The notebook's phrase for the result is "the standardised stillness sold as peace."

The first instinct is to describe normalisation as a kind of inversion — a good force (order, coordination, shared language) that flips into its shadow (conformity, control). But that description turned out to be wrong, or at least incomplete. Normalisation is not a flip. It is a **dosage problem**.

Norms are not bad. A norm is a force like any other, and in small doses it *adds* — it gives a group a shared floor to stand on without telling anyone what to build. The damage happens not when a norm appears but when it *displaces*: when the shared floor rises until there is no room left for the individual force it was supposed to support. The question to ask of any norm, any process, any "best practice" is therefore not "is this good or bad?" but **"does this add without displacing, or does it displace entirely?"** → [[Additive vs Displacing Forces]]

Two further distinctions follow.

**Authorship versus imposition.** A norm you wrote — for yourself, for your team — is a norm you can revise. A norm imposed on you is a norm you can only obey or break. The same rule can be additive when authored and displacing when imposed. This is why sovereignty matters to normalisation: the *source* of the norm changes its dose. → [[Authorship vs Imposition]]

**Capability versus goods.** A system that shares *capability* — teaches, equips, opens — creates a flywheel: each recipient becomes a source. A system that shares *goods* — hands over the finished thing — creates dependency, and dependency is the quietest form of displacement. → [[Capability-sharing vs Goods-sharing]]

## Material (stated)
- Anti-normalisation: modern society, culture, and business flatten individuality and erode genuine human tension. → [[Anti-normalisation]]
- Normalisation is a dosage problem — not inversion, but progressive displacement.
- Sharpened alongside: authorship vs imposition of norms; additive vs displacing forces; capability-sharing vs goods-sharing; the structural role of exit.
- Tension is suppressed by normalisation, not created by the framework.

## Blanks

> [!blank]
> **What is the *dose-response curve*?** If normalisation is dosage, is there a threshold, a gradient, or a cliff? Does it differ by scale (a family can absorb more shared norm than a nation)? A worked example at two scales would make the chapter.

> [!blank]
> **Is "normalisation" the right word?** It carries a statistical and a sociological meaning, and the framework uses something closer to *standardisation* or *flattening*. The notebook uses "standardised stillness". Tom to decide the term of art.

> [!claude]
> The dosage reframe has a consequence for the values: if normalisation fails by dosage, do some "X without Y" pairs also fail by dosage rather than inversion? "Optimisation without Suppression" reads as a dosage risk (a little systematising helps; too much suffocates), whereas "Strength without Cruelty" reads as an inversion risk (strength doesn't *gradually* become cruelty; it flips when it stops protecting). If that's right, the grammar of the values may need a second axis. This is [[OQ-01 Inversion vs Dosage Risks]] and it is probably the most important open question in the book.

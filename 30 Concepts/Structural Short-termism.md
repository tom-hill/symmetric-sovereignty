# Structural Short-termism

## Definition (as held)
Democracies under-invest in the long term **by architecture, not by negligence**. The mechanism is specific and repeatable:

1. **Electoral cycles run 4–5 years; the important investments pay back over 15–30.**
2. **Costs are visible and immediate; benefits are diffuse and delayed.**
3. **Austerity is politically legible; complexity is not.** "How will you pay for it?" is the first question asked, almost never "what is the long-term return?"
4. **Violent policy discontinuity.** A 15-year programme started by one party is cut by the next, who are blamed for the collapse, and replaced by the first, who restart it. The churn is itself the failure mechanism.

Reinforced by: politicians who genuinely believe the household-budget metaphor, treasuries institutionally committed to deficit reduction, media that frames spending as cost, ideology that wants small government for its own sake, and the alignment of class interest with all of the above. Not a conspiracy — power and interest self-perpetuating.

> A system faithfully doing what its architecture tells it to do, which happens to be the wrong thing.

## Why it belongs here
It is the clearest worked example of [[Restraint in Machinery]] failing — and of its converse, that structures produce behaviour regardless of the values of the people inside them. It is also the civilisational-scale version of the VC critique: both describe capital optimising for a horizon shorter than the thing it is funding. → [[Part 8 — The Model for Modern Business is Broken]]

## Tom's proposed correctives
Recorded as proposals, not settled positions: a sovereign fund held at the Bank of England; removal of first-past-the-post so no government is elected without a majority; **political remuneration tied to long-horizon outcomes**, so that a party that loses the next cycle still carries an interest in the policy surviving; and education reform as the hardest and most necessary change.
